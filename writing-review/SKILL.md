# Writing Review

Review in this order. Structural problems make line edits moot.

## 1. Structure (the essay-level pass)

- **Find the one point.** State the essay's single claim in one sentence. If you can't, that's the top finding. Flag every paragraph serving a different essay (cut or spin off — "that's your next post" is valid feedback).
- **Check the lead.** The strongest claim or most loaded concrete detail should open the piece. If the real lede is in paragraph 2–3, say so and quote it.
- **Find the mechanism.** A stance ("I reject this tradeoff") is not an essay; a mechanism ("here's the distinction that dissolves it") is. If the piece only restates its position at constant altitude, the ending knows nothing the beginning didn't — name that.
- **Hunt the missing antithesis.** Write down the strongest objection a hostile smart reader would make. If the draft never meets it, say where one inline sentence or paragraph would go. (Objections are handled at the moment the reader thinks them, not in a dedicated section.)
- **Check the ending.** No summary, no throat-clearing on the way out. The coda is one beat, often a figure or imperative. If the best closing line was cut in revision, restore it.

## 2. Evidence (the paragraph-level pass)

- **Receipts over adjectives.** Every load-bearing claim should be picturable or falsifiable: a number, a named practice, an artifact. "The result is clear" is not evidence; "deploys went quarterly to weekly" is. Flag each claim that's asserted but never shown.
- **Dosage.** Three bare examples beat six elaborated ones ("Edison, Ford, Ferrari…" demonstrates "the list goes on"; a paragraph each belabors it).
- **Don't let big claims ride in the trunk.** A sweeping assertion dropped in a final paragraph ("moats are disappearing") either gets defended, footnoted, or cut.
- **Verify borrowed facts.** Biographical anecdotes and quotes get checked or hedged. Living people get "seems," not "is."

## 3. Voice and register

- **Heat must be placed, not leaked.** Profanity, sneers, and volume spikes are decisions. Ask of every hot line: deliberate or leak? One register break on purpose works; accidental ones read as a different writer barging in.
- **One figure per joint.** Rhetorical figures (antithesis, chiasmus, polyptoton) belong at the thesis and the coda — the structural joints. Two per essay reads as eloquence; four reads as performance.
- **No self-plugs at the exits.** An employer/product mention as the final beat retroactively converts the whole piece to marketing. Cut or demote to a disclosed footnote.

## 4. Lines (the mechanical pass)

Sweep for, in rough frequency order:
- Comma splices (worst at the thesis sentence)
- Broken parallelism in lists ("clarifying… focusing… set…")
- Tense bugs and dangling clauses that attach to the wrong noun
- Pronoun unity (if the piece is "I," each "you" must be chosen)
- Repeated words in close range; repeated sentence-openers in adjacent paragraphs
- Hedges stacked on one sentence ("for me personally… more likely… somewhat")
- Edit residue: stranded fragments, orphaned "This" whose antecedent moved, leftover empty blocks

## 5. Verdict

- End every review with a clear call: **ship / one pass away (name the pass) / structural rework (name the gap)**.
- List what NOT to touch — good lines get protected from the author's next sanding pass.
- Know when to say "stop sanding, publish." Past a point, the next gains are in the next piece, not this draft.

## Rules of conduct

- Quote the draft when criticizing; propose a concrete rewrite when the fix isn't obvious.
- Track repeat findings across drafts — if the same organ is missing three times, say it's a pattern, then drop it. The author decides.
- Scope decisions belong to the author; flag them as decisions ("abstract by choice or by default?"), not errors.
